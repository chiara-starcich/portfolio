HealthBlocks

Walking together, not just for money

A step-challenge app redesign: a community and a second currency, hearts, to bring in more women and keep people moving.

eHealth, app + reward system TU Delft, eHealth Design | With Veronique Serné | 2025 to 26

The situation

HealthBlocks pays you to walk: join a step challenge and win back your fee plus a share of the pool.

My role
  • Literature review, SWOT & stakeholder analysis
  • 2 questionnaires
  • Personas
  • User journeys
  • Hearts system (co-designed)
  • UI refinement
Result

A second reward next to money: hearts, earned by supporting others. Money still comes only from walking.

Methods
  • Literature review
  • SWOT analysis
  • Stakeholder analysis
  • 2 questionnaires
  • Personas
  • System diagrams
  • Sketching

Challenge

Built on competition and money. Mostly men joined.

Competition and money are traditionally masculine motivators.

But motivation depends on identity, social context and life experience, not gender. So we designed for women aged 20 to 45 without stereotypes.

How might we

How might we grow HealthBlocks and attract more women, while keeping people active for the long term?

The solution

Introducing the new HealthBlocks

Same challenges, reframed as a shared journey: summits instead of rankings, groups instead of leaderboards.

01

Your daily summit

Daily goal and Summit steps on top, your friends and groups just below.

02

Choose a summit

A recommended pool explained up front, then popular and community pools.

03

Progress together

What the group achieved, your groups, and what friends did today.

Veronique designed the first UI. I refined it, partly with Google Stitch, and we moved from black to a bright palette.

Define

The app had money and challenges. It had no community.

Key user: the socially motivated

Lara, 32, architect in London

She loves doing things with others and wants to move more, but only keeps it up when others are involved.

Money doesn’t motivate her. A supportive group does.

Money gets people started. A community keeps them going.

Process

Design vision

“To increase user growth by facilitating a sense of community.”

Money for moving, hearts for helping: social value supports physical effort, it never replaces it.

What reshaped it
  • FailedMoney and competition alone: mostly men joined, and losers dropped out→ Groups and summits instead of leaderboards
  • ConstraintHearts can’t be cashed in, or people would earn without walking→ Money comes only from steps
  • ConstraintThe paid step challenges had to stay→ Same challenges, reframed as summits

Outcome

From a list of pools to a summit you choose.

Summit pools

One recommended pool to join in one tap, then nature, popular and member-made pools.

Community

No leaderboards: your hearts, your groups and what you achieved together.

Communities don’t form because people can talk. They form because people need each other to make progress.

What I learned
  • Designing for women doesn’t mean designing “for women”: motivation depends on context, not gender.

Unifood

Lunch delivered to campus

Order lunch from the canteen, bakeries and delis, delivered by one rider to campus at 1 pm, with discounts that cut food waste.

Service + app design Team of 4 | Università di Ferrara | 2023

The situation

In Ferrara, students of architecture and many other faculties have one hour for lunch, and the canteen is too far.

My role
  • Survey, interviews & field research
  • Actors map & storyboard
  • Paper prototypes & testing
  • Final UI design
Result

A tested service and app with student discounts that also cut food waste: cheaper if you order in advance, or choose the day-before leftovers.

Methods
  • Survey
  • Interviews
  • Design brief
  • Actors map
  • Field research
  • Storyboard
  • Paper prototypes
  • Prototype testing (4 students)

Challenge

The brief

One hour for lunch, a canteen too far away.

Most students commute, so they buy lunch near the department.

They wanted healthier, more varied options, and to spend less money.

Two goals from the start
Lunch on time

Delivered to campus within the one-hour break.

Less food waste

Sellers’ leftovers from the day before sold at a student discount.

The solution

Introducing Unifood

Order from the canteen, bakery, fruit shop or deli. One rider collects everything and delivers it to pickup points on campus at 1 pm.

01

Order in three taps

From the start screen to today’s discounts, straight to your meal plan.

02

Track, then reorder

Switch between today’s order and your history.

03

Collect it on campus

The rider drops lunch at pickup points on campus: choose the one nearest your lecture.

Screens and flows designed by our team in 2023. I designed the final UI in 2026 with Google Stitch, keeping the same flows.

Define

40students surveyed
4sellers visited: canteen, bakery, deli, fruit shop
7groups mapped, from commuters to staff

The problem wasn’t the food: one hour isn’t enough to go and get it.

Process

1 · First sketches

↓

2 · Mid fidelity in Figma
We iterated
  • Calendar and order history unclear→ Orders split into two tabs: upcoming and history
  • “How do I get the discount?”→ The discount explained on the welcome and home screens
  • Icons didn’t look tappable→ Clearer, tappable icons, and fewer words

Outcome

The service: how an order travels
  1. 1Pick a seller

    Canteen, deli, bakery or fruit shop

  2. 2Pay in the app

    Discount if ordered in advance, or for yesterday’s leftovers

  3. 3Choose a pickup point

    On campus, near your lecture

  4. 4One rider delivers

    Everything, at 1 pm

On time

Lunch arrives within the one-hour break.

Cheaper

Student discounts, and canteen and bakery prices that delivery apps don’t offer.

Less food waste

Leftovers from the day before are sold, and advance orders let sellers cook the right amount.

Good for sellers

One collection round, and digital orders for shops that had none.

What I learned
  • Sometimes the solution is a service, not a screen: one rider at 1 pm mattered more than any feature.
  • Talking to the sellers shaped the concept as much as talking to the students.

Reliance Toolbox

A method and clickable toolbox to study how doctors rely on AI

An interactive toolbox for Philips usability designers who test medical products with users.

Digital tool for experts Solo graduation project | Philips × UMC Utrecht × TU Delft | Feb to Aug 2026

The situation

AI helps doctors diagnose but is never 100% right. Usability designers had no method to study how much doctors rely on it.

The field lab: a real hospital ward used to test new medical technology with real data, without the answers affecting patient care.

My role
  • Research: 7 expert interviews
  • Concept & method
  • Expert testing (2 rounds)
  • UX/UI design
  • Figma prototype, refined with Claude
What I designed

Two things: a method to study how doctors’ reliance on AI changes over time in the field lab, and a clickable toolbox that helps usability designers apply it. The method comes from my research; the toolbox was shaped by two rounds of testing with Philips usability experts.

Methods
  • 7 expert interviews
  • 2 rounds of expert feedback
  • Prototyping in Figma

Challenge

A new colleague in the reading room. It is never 100% right.

AI can be wrong. What matters is what doctors do with it, and that changes over time.

Long hospital studies are hard to run.

One lab test can’t show how reliance changes over time.

How might we

How might designers test, over time and in a real hospital, how doctors rely on AI?

The solution

Introducing the Reliance Toolbox

Learn the basics, or jump straight to a table of tools you can filter.

Tested with Philips usability experts in two rounds. The team now plans to use it in a real usability study.

The core

Compare and filter tools

Every tool side by side on 9 criteria, grouped before, during and after the test.

Define

Seven interviews rewrote the brief.

Field lab limits
  • Access to patients and staff is limited
  • Doctors have very little time
  • Measuring must not disturb their work
  • Privacy approval needed; data stays anonymous
  • Real data, but answers don’t affect patients
What changes reliance over time
  • Fatigue
  • AI errors
  • Workload
  • Growing familiarity

Designers needed guidance and suitable tools, not another tool.

Process

Four structures. Experts picked the hub.

5

experts tested my concepts: 4 Philips usability designers, 1 researcher

2

rounds of expert testing

1

real hospital test ward studied from the inside, plus research papers

The hub in Figma

Insights → design decisions
  • Field lab visitDoctors have very little time, and measuring must not disturb their work→ A table comparing every tool on 9 criteria, so designers pick what fits the ward
  • 7 interviewsReliance changes over time: fatigue, AI errors, workload, familiarity→ Tools grouped before, during and after the test, to follow change over time
  • 7 interviewsDesigners didn’t need another tool; they needed guidance to choose→ A method that guides the choice, plus a toolbox of existing tools
  • Expert testing, round 1Experts wanted freedom, not fixed steps→ A hub with four sections instead of a fixed sequence
  • Expert testing, round 2A real example helped more than instructions→ A one-page example study

Let designers pick their own tools, with guidance and a real example, not fixed steps.

Outcome

What I learned
  • Experts don’t want to be guided step by step: they want freedom, with a good example.
  • Seven interviews can rewrite a brief. Listening first kept me from building the wrong method.

DOC

A calming companion for ER patients

A device for ER patients that hangs on the bed rail and explains their vitals through screen, sound and touch.

Sight, sound, touch: product + screen Team of 4 | Erasmus MC | 3 months Cover render by a teammate

Render

The situation

ER patients wait alone for hours next to a monitor that beeps, but that they can’t read.

My role
  • Test design & user testing (led): card sort, VR sound test, texture test, final test
  • Sound design
  • Ideation
  • Screen design
  • Render refinement
Result

In the final test, people felt watched over and calmer, and showed us what to fix: meters and colours.

Methods
  • Card sorting
  • VR sound test
  • Texture test

Challenge

Two hours, one beeping machine, nobody to ask.

The machine meant to protect them scares them.

Where it hurts
  • Screens and beeps about their health raise anxiety
  • Hours in the same position are frustrating and scary
  • Medical procedures add more anxiety
How might we

How might we help ER patients feel safe and in control while they wait?

The solution

Introducing DOC

Hang it on the bed rail or lift it off: squeeze, touch and tap to see how your body is doing.

Define

Patients didn’t want more numbers. They wanted to know they were okay.

Process

Two ideas, one made it to the end.

✓ Chosen

Concept 1: a vital monitor patients understand

Three early screen styles, tested with patients.

Dropped

Concept 2: a recovery-exercise game

Wall exercises tracked by a camera. Interviewees said they’d soon get bored, so it wouldn’t ease anxiety.

Testing the prototype

Final test: 4 people, mock patient room

Worked: they felt watched over; icons, short texts and a kind tone on bad news calmed them

Didn’t: the green smiley looked like a sick emoji; the meter and all-green colours confused people

Touch test

Bubble and ridged textures, taken from anti-stress toys on the market, were preferred: touching them distracted the body, stuck in bed, and eased anxiety.

Insights → design decisions
  • Card sort, visual testPatients didn’t want more numbers; they wanted to know they were okay→ An avatar and simple meters instead of raw numbers
  • VR sound testBeeps raised anxiety; calm sounds lowered it→ Relaxing sounds; beeping only in an emergency
  • Touch testHours stuck in bed made people restless→ Bubble and ridged textures to squeeze and hold
  • Final testA kind tone on bad news calmed people→ A gentler message when a value drops: “You’re being looked after”
  • Final testThe green smiley looked sick and the meters confused people→ Next step: clearer meters and colours

Show only what patients care about, explain it calmly, and tell them when they’re okay, without hiding anything.

Outcome

How a patient uses it

  1. 1

    On the rail

    Hangs next to the hospital monitor

  2. 2

    Lift it off

    Shaped to be held in both hands

  3. 3

    Touch it

    Squishy cushion, bubbly grips, ridged sides: textures that calm and help focus

How DOC behaves

  1. 1

    All good

    The avatar greets the patient, green meters, calm, relaxing sound

  2. 2

    A value drops

    A gentler message and a yellow note: “You’re being looked after”

  3. 3

    Emergency

    Only now does the beeping start

The back, home and forward buttons flip through heart rate, oxygen and temperature.

What I learned
  • Tone of voice, sound and touch calmed people more than data did.
  • Feeling in control calmed people: knowing how they are, even when it isn’t good news.
  • Testing one sense at a time made a complex, multi-sensory product manageable.
